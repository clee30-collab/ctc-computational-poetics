Interactive Stories

My Boyfriend Came Back From The War, 1996 is a browser-based net-art piece created by Russian artist Olia Lialina in 1996. The hyperlinks all split into smaller frames and sometimes GIFs that unveils a nonlinear story about a couple reuniting after the war. Olia calls the work a “netfilm” and is meant to be cinematic and narrative just by using HTML code, hypertext, and interaction.

Claude Clotsky Love or Lust, 1997 it was work commissioned for Dia’s Artist Web Projects, the longest-running U.S. program of its kind (launched in 1995). The work presents an endless series of absurd, contradictory questions in the style of pop magazine quizzes, and each click forces the user to pick between two options. In the 1990s many theorists celebrated hypertext for its potential to create open-ended, reader-driven narratives. Closky’s piece makes it unclear whether your choices matter at all, and the quiz never delivers a result or conclusion. Rather than offering the freedom promised by cyber-utopianism, the work recasts online “freedom” as endless choosing within a fixed set of options. It reduces desire to dating clichés and consumer-style choices, and it exposes how limiting those false binaries are for expressing a self.

Queers in Love at the End of the World, 2013 is a short hypertext game Anna Anthropy made in Twine for the 2013 Ludum Dare game jam. Each play lasts only ten seconds, as a timer counts down while the player clicks highlighted text to choose how to spend those final moments with their lover. However you play, the world always ends. Scholar Claudia Lo praised its take on queer temporality, drawing on José Esteban Muñoz’s Cruising Utopia, and The Guardian’s Cara Ellison praised how well it captures a life of transience. In 2018 it was featured in the Victoria and Albert Museum’s “Videogames: Design/Play/Disrupt” exhibition. Hyperlinks can be thought of as “desire lines” that break up chronological order. It reflects how queer identity forms non-linearly.
