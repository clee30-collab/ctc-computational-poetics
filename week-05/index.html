Collective Hypertext

Hypertext = text containing links to other text or media, enhancing navigation and information retrieval.
Hyperlinks = are the links embedded in hypertext, directing users to different sections, pages, or resources.

The concept of the Hyperlink started with the memex machine. It was a theoretical idea invented by Vannevar Bush who was an American engineer and scientist. He first introduced the memex machine in his 1945 article “As We May Think.” It was supposed to be a machine that could store large amounts of information and allow users to connect documents through associative trails rather than sorting an entire index. It formed the idea that information can be linked together. It directly inspired Ted Nelson who later coined the actual term hypertext in 1965. And then at Brown, Andries van Dam’s teams built HES (1967) and FRESS (1968). FRESS (File Retrieval and Editing System) It was the first hypertext system to run on available commercial hardware and had a simpler interface designed for a general university audience. It supported the first scholarly online community in a hyperlinked poetry class and was said to foreshadow wikis, blogs and other sharing documents.

Then in 1987, Apple released Hypercard. It is a software application and development kit for Apple Macintosh and Apple IIGS computers. HyperCard allowed users to create interactive applications by linking digital cards containing text, images, buttons, and scripts. Its main innovation was making programming accessible to people with little coding knowledge, allowing many to build their own tools, games, and multimedia experiences. It was eventually discontinued, but the HyperCard influenced the foundation of the World Wide Web and JavaScript. It really showed the importance of making accessible creative tools that empower people to experiment with technology and become creators.

Ward Cunningham created WikiWikiWeb, the first public wiki on March 25, 1995. Users could create, edit and link web pages directly in a browser. Wikipedia launched on January 15, 2001 as a faster, open alternative to the expert-reviewed Nupedia. Wikis created “active readers” meaning they can choose a path through the text and because wikis are editable the collective knowledge is always nonlinear. Wikis are what the memex imagined, linked trails to different pages of information.

The Garden of Forking Paths is a book written by Argentine writer Jorge Luis Borges. His vision of "forking paths" has been cited as inspiration by many new media scholars/artists within the field of hypertext fiction. The novel has themes of time, decisions = consequences, and duty vs. morality. Its story is mainly centered on the idea of infinite branching timelines. Borges questions if there is a single, predetermined reality and believes that every choice creates alternative possibilities. The story’s main character is Yu Tsun, a Chinese spy working for Germany during World War I. Yu Tsun’s eventual success shows how achieving a goal always comes at the expense of something else.

Tear Down the Wall: Hypertext and Participatory Narratives, is an electronic literature exhibition held alongside the ACM Hypertext 2019 conference in Hof, Germany. The conference took its “tear down the wall” theme from the anniversary of the Berlin Wall to reunite different hypertext communities, including electronic literature. It celebrates the first ACM Hypertext conference in 1987 and ita prediction that computers would bring new ways of writing and reading. The exhibition mixes early hypertext work with newer experimental work. Judy Malloy, Mark Bernstein and John McDaid all revisit or extend their early work, with McDaid’s We Knew the Glass Man built in Twine. Mark Amerika’s Grammatron is an early piece of net art that critiqued the rise of linking and clicking. Other works push hypertext beyond text: Mez Breeze’s VR and spatial storytelling, Serge Bouchardon’s sensor-based smartphone piece fred:-), which also started in Twine, and John Barber and Greg Philbrook’s Sound Spheres, which links sounds instead of words. The show closes with J-B-W-E-L-L’s satirical PDF hypertext novel critiquing neoliberalism.
